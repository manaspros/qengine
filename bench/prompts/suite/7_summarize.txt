Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor, yet they support roughly a quarter of all known marine species. The reef itself is built by tiny animals called polyps, which secrete calcium carbonate skeletons and live in partnership with single-celled algae known as zooxanthellae. The algae live inside the polyps' tissues, where they photosynthesize and pass most of the sugars they produce to their hosts; in return they receive shelter and nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus from the coral's waste. This partnership is what allows reefs to thrive in clear tropical waters that are otherwise poor in nutrients.

The partnership is fragile. When water temperatures stay one or two degrees Celsius above the usual summer maximum for several weeks, the algae's photosynthetic machinery is damaged and begins to produce reactive oxygen compounds. The coral responds by expelling the algae, which removes the source of its color and most of its food, an event called bleaching. Bleached corals are not dead, and if temperatures fall quickly many recover their algae within weeks or months. Prolonged or repeated bleaching, however, leaves corals starved and vulnerable to disease, and large areas can die.

Mass bleaching events have become more frequent since the 1980s, with global events recorded in 1998, 2010, 2014 to 2017 and again in recent years. Researchers monitor reefs with satellite measurements of sea surface temperature, aerial surveys and divers counting coral cover along fixed transects. Some reefs appear more tolerant than others: corals in places with naturally variable temperatures, or those hosting heat-tolerant strains of algae, often bleach less. Scientists are testing interventions such as breeding heat-tolerant corals, shading reefs during heatwaves and transplanting nursery-grown fragments, but these approaches are expensive and work only at small scales. Most researchers agree that the long-term survival of reefs depends on limiting ocean warming, while local measures such as reducing pollution and overfishing can improve how well reefs recover between heat events.
